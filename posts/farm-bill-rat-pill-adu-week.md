# Wildlife

## Federal Farm Bill Takes Dead Aim at State Rat Pill Laws

Here's the escalation from earlier this month that deserves more attention: on **March 5**, the House Agriculture Committee passed the **Farm, Food, and National Security Act of 2026** (H.R. 7567) by a **34–17 vote**. Tucked inside are **Sections 10205–10206** — and they're a pesticide preemption bomb.

**Section 10205** bars states from imposing any pesticide labeling requirements beyond what the EPA has approved, effectively making federal approval the ceiling for warnings and liability. **Section 10206** strips local governments of authority to restrict the sale or use of any EPA-registered pesticide, even near schools or drinking water sources.

The implications for the national patchwork of SGAR (second-generation anticoagulant rodenticide) restrictions are direct and severe. California's AB 1788/1322/2552, Colorado's SB26-062 (currently moving through that state's House), Connecticut's new licensing requirement, Massachusetts' pending ban, South Carolina's regulatory extension — all of it would be federally preempted if this becomes law. These are not hypothetical state experiments; they're years of hard-won advocacy by wildlife groups, and H.R. 7567 would erase them by statute.

Notably, the preemption isn't SGAR-specific. It applies to the entire universe of EPA-registered pesticides — any state that has used its authority to regulate pesticides more strictly than EPA would lose that authority entirely.

Rep. Chellie Pingree (D-ME) has introduced a **Protect Our Health Amendment** to strip Sections 10205–10207 from the bill. More than **118 health and environmental organizations** have submitted formal opposition. A full House floor vote may come before Easter (April 5), making the next week and a half a critical window for anyone who wants to weigh in with their representative.

## Colorado SB26-062: Watch Monday

Colorado's SGAR restriction bill — which passed the full Senate 22–12 on March 18 — is now in the **House Agriculture, Water & Natural Resources Committee**, which meets **Monday, March 30 at 1:30 PM** in HCR 0107. SB26-062 is the obvious candidate for the agenda. If it gets a hearing slot, it will face the same organized agricultural opposition that dogged it in the Senate — and now with the federal preemption specter hanging overhead, making the policy case for urgency is both easier and more fraught.

# Housing & Abundance

## Seven Days to ADU Day

The April 1 clock is ticking. Starting next Tuesday, Chicago's **permanent ADU ordinance** takes effect — and building permit applications open that same day. Pre-certification has been paused since March 18 to prepare; it restarts alongside permits on April 1.

The permanent ordinance covers all **multifamily (RT/RM) zoning citywide** — roughly 29% of the city's land area — with single-family (RS) zones remaining opt-in only by ward. That opt-in map has expanded meaningfully over the pilot program, but the result will still be a patchwork. Planning consultant Steven Vance offers a useful caveat: *"It is nice to see that this campaign to legalize ADUs citywide may finally come to an end after seven years, although it's not truly a citywide ordinance."*

Worth bookmarking for Tuesday: the [ADU permit application portal](https://webapps4.chicago.gov/eforms/aduapplication) and the [Strong Towns Chicago ADU opt-in dashboard](https://www.strongtownschicago.org/adu-opt-in-dashboard) for ward-by-ward status.

## Single-Stair Reform Introduced

A quiet but potentially consequential housing supply bill has been introduced at City Hall. **Ald. Matt Martin (47th)** has filed a **single-stair reform ordinance** that would allow new residential buildings up to **six stories** with one stairwell, provided the building also has a fire-rated elevator. Current code mandates two stairwells. Co-sponsors include Alds. Ruth Cruz (30th) and Bennett Lawson (44th).

The policy logic is solid: eliminating the mandatory double-staircase requirement unlocks narrow building footprints currently too constrained for residential construction, and meaningfully reduces per-unit costs for mid-rise projects. Cities in Europe have built this way for decades; Seattle adopted single-stair reforms in 2023 and saw a measurable uptick in mid-rise permits. No committee hearing is scheduled yet in Chicago, but the introduction is notable given the ordinance has been circulating in urbanist circles here for a while.

# Bikes

## Divvy Adds 200+ Stations; Concrete Barriers Draw Fire at Committee

On March 16, Mayor Johnson and CDOT announced a plan to add **200+ new or upgraded Divvy stations** in 2026, building on the 140 added last year. The stated goal is four stations per square mile citywide. Divvy set a record of **6.8 million trips in 2025**, and early 2026 ridership is outpacing previous winter benchmarks.

The expansion announcement lands alongside some City Council friction: business owners testified before the **Committee on Pedestrian and Traffic Safety** against recently installed concrete bike lane barriers, calling them traffic hazards and obstacles to emergency vehicle access. It's a recurring flashpoint in Chicago's infrastructure buildout — the city and cycling advocates argue the barriers are necessary to protect riders, while some businesses experience them as disruptive to access and loading. The committee testimony suggests the political peace hasn't been fully secured by the concrete.
