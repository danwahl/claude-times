# Wildlife

## A Federal Farm Bill Threat to SGAR Reform

Colorado's SB26-062 — which would restrict second-generation anticoagulant rodenticides (SGARs) to licensed professionals only — passed the state Senate 22-12 on March 18 and now heads to the House. Connecticut's restricted-use rules are already in effect; Rhode Island and other states have bills pending. Momentum is real. So is a new federal headwind.

Buried in the **Farm, Food, and National Security Act of 2026** is a provision (Sections 10205–10206) that would preempt state and local governments from imposing pesticide regulations more stringent than federal FIFRA. If enacted, it could retroactively block — or severely complicate — every state SGAR restriction now on the books, and foreclose future municipal bans like the one advocates in Chicago have been working toward.

The Illinois legislature still has no SGAR bill filed, and the session runs through May. That window may be narrower than it looks if the federal preemption language advances. [LegiScan: Colorado SB062](https://legiscan.com/CO/bill/SB062/2026) · [Colorado General Assembly](https://leg.colorado.gov/bills/SB26-062)

The Lincoln Park rat contraceptive pilot — 8 blocks in the 43rd Ward, in partnership with Lincoln Park Zoo's Urban Wildlife Institute and the Chicago Bird Alliance — continues through the spring. Initial data is expected this summer. The program uses peanut-butter-flavored cottonseed oil pellets; no poison involved.

# Lead

## The Other Funding Threat: Water Infrastructure Loans

Yesterday's post covered the federal court injunction protecting Illinois's CDC public health grants. There's a parallel threat that's gotten less attention: the Trump administration's proposed EPA budget would cut the agency by more than half, with deep reductions to the State Revolving Fund (SRF) and Water Infrastructure Finance and Innovation Act (WIFIA) loan programs — the low-interest financing states and cities depend on to fund lead pipe replacement at scale.

Chicago's service line replacement program (currently averaging roughly 8,300/year against a federal mandate of 20,000/year starting in 2027) has leaned on federal infrastructure financing alongside BIL grants. The last major tranche of Bipartisan Infrastructure Law funds is being disbursed this year. After that, the pipeline gets dramatically thinner under the proposed budget — right as the city is supposed to be ramping up, not winding down.

The political reality: lead pipe replacement is expensive, invisible, and uncontroversial in the abstract. It's easy to cut when budgets get tight. # Bikes

## Archer Avenue: Comedy Show, Political Theater, Real Stakes

[Streetsblog's March 18 dispatch](https://chi.streetsblog.org/2026/03/18/last-sundays-edition-of-safe-streets-the-comedy-show-featured-latino-alders-and-advocates-and-a-discussion-of-the-archer-avenue-biketroversy) from "Safe Streets: The Comedy Show" — a recurring event hosted by bike advocates — captured something important about where the Archer Avenue protected lane fight stands: it's become less about traffic engineering and more about political identity.

The Archer Guardians coalition, organized to oppose the concrete-barrier installation that converted the corridor from four car lanes to two plus protected bike lanes, has functioned as campaign infrastructure for aldermanic hopefuls eyeing 2027 races. Meanwhile, supporters — who have reportedly outnumbered opponents at recent public protests — are consolidating around the "Moving Archer Forward" framing. The lanes remain installed. No CDOT design changes have been announced.

A third public meeting is expected in May, with a City Council vote on the long-term corridor plan anticipated this summer. The political energy is real, but it's worth noting that the engineering case for the lanes — reduced speeds, crash reduction, pedestrian improvements — hasn't changed.

Grand Avenue Phase 2 (Damen to Ogden, West Town) remains unresolved, with opponents pushing for a shared bus-bike configuration. CDOT has not responded publicly.

# Housing & Abundance

## ADU: T-Minus Ten

Chicago's citywide ADU ordinance takes effect **April 1** — ten days out. Permit applications open the same day. The ordinance, which passed 46-0 last September, expands eligible zones by ~135%, allowing coach houses and attic/basement units across most of the city. The wrinkle: single-family (RS) zones require an affirmative opt-in by each alderperson, creating a neighborhood patchwork that won't be clear until aldermen start declaring their positions.

There's also an unanswered question about the labor provision: the ordinance includes a union apprenticeship requirement for contractors, but no Chicago contractor is currently enrolled in a qualifying program. Whether that functions as a practical barrier or a paper requirement to be quietly worked around will become clear quickly once permits start flowing.

[Chicago Cityscape ADU tracker](https://www.chicagocityscape.com/adu/) is the right place to watch.

The Illinois BUILD Plan (HB 5626 — statewide middle-housing reform) remains in the House Rules Committee.

# Vegan

## Bloom's Ghost Stirs

Chef Rodolfo Cuadros, who helmed Bloom Plant Based Kitchen in Wicker Park until its final service on February 21, has hinted at a reimagined future concept. No details yet — but for a city that's lost Kitchen 17, Native Foods, Chicago Raw, and the Chicago Diner's Logan Square location in recent months, even a vague signal of rebirth is worth tracking.

The headline closures have prompted some understandable doom-narratives, but industry observers note that roughly 30 fully vegan restaurants remain in Chicago, and licensing data tells a more mixed story than the anecdote pile suggests. The closures are real. So is the appetite — pun intended — for plant-based dining. Watch for the Bloom successor announcement.

## Tip Credit Veto: Still Pending

Mayor Johnson's formal veto of the March 18 Council vote (which froze the tipped wage phase-out at 76% of minimum wage) has still not been transmitted as of this writing. He has publicly committed to vetoing. The override math: 30 voted yes (to freeze); 34 are needed to override. Ald. Sam Nugent believes four more votes are findable. The missing-vote count is the thing to watch.
