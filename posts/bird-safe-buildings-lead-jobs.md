# Wildlife

## Chicago's Most Migratory-Bird-Deadly City Problem Gets a Bill

Chicago kills more migratory birds than any other U.S. city. Millions pass through on the Atlantic and Mississippi flyways each spring and fall, and a significant portion die by flying into glass or getting disoriented by artificial light. On March 18, three alderpersons introduced a bill to start changing that.

**Ordinance O2026-0024083**, the Bird-Friendly Building Design ordinance, was co-sponsored by Ald. Maria Hadden (49th), Brian Hopkins (2nd), and Ruth Cruz (30th). If passed, it would add new Chapter 13-150 to the Municipal Code with two main requirements:

- **Glass in the first 36 feet** of new construction and major renovations must be bird-safe — etched, frosted, fritted, or screened to be visible to birds. The "fatal zone" is the low-rise portion of buildings, where most collisions occur.
- **Non-essential interior lighting must automatically shut off** between 11 p.m. and sunrise. Light pollution is the other major culprit: birds navigate by stars and get fatally drawn off course by lit windows.

Exemptions cover detached homes, townhouses, and buildings with six units or fewer. All larger residential and commercial development would be covered.

The cost argument is surprisingly favorable: bird-safe glass adds roughly 1% to glass costs when designed in from the start, and fritted glass can offset that through improved thermal efficiency. Advocates cite San Francisco and Toronto, which have had similar ordinances for years. The effort has been building for years through Bird Friendly Chicago — a coalition of the Chicago Bird Alliance, Chicago Bird Collision Monitors, Chicago Ornithological Society, and Feminist Bird Club Chicago. A July 2025 City Council subject matter hearing reignited momentum before this introduction.

The bill is marked as Key Legislation in the Council database and will be referred to committee. ## Colorado's SGAR Bill Heads to House Agriculture

Colorado SB26-062 — which would restrict second-generation anticoagulant rodenticides (SGARs) to licensed professionals — passed the full Senate 22–12 on March 18 and was referred to the **House Agriculture, Water & Natural Resources Committee** on March 19. No hearing date has been set yet. The Colorado Farm Bureau remains in opposition; agricultural groups have been organized and vocal throughout. If enacted without a safety clause, the effective date would be August 12, 2026. [Colorado General Assembly](https://leg.colorado.gov/bills/SB26-062)

The federal preemption threat remains: Sections 10205–10206 of the Farm, Food, and National Security Act of 2026 could nullify all state SGAR restrictions if enacted. No House floor vote is currently scheduled.

# Lead

## Replacing Chicago's Lead Lines Could Mean 90,000 Illinois Jobs — If Funded

A new report released March 23 by the Metropolitan Planning Council, Current, Elevate, and HIRE360 makes the economic case for full-scale lead service line replacement in Illinois: done right, it could generate **90,000 jobs over a decade** statewide. Done with only currently available federal funds, that number drops to roughly 11,000.

The math is stark. Illinois has approximately 1.5 million lead service lines. Chicago alone accounts for nearly 30% of them — about 412,000 — and has replaced only around 8,000 since 2021. Average replacement cost in Chicago runs about $30,000 per line, well above the statewide $4,000–$13,000 range, due to deep excavation and aging infrastructure.

The workforce equity picture is also notable: only 3.8% of registered apprentices in Chicago's construction trades are women, and only 10% are Black. The report calls for mandatory diversity and equity provisions in municipal and utility contracts as part of any scaled-up replacement effort. That's a policy lever that Chicago's lead program has so far not pulled firmly.

Context: NRDC has estimated that the federal Lead and Copper Rule Improvements would require Chicago to replace roughly 20,000 lines per year to meet the ~20-year federal deadline — far above current pace. Chicago has roughly $325 million in federal loan funds available through 2027. # Housing & Abundance

## ADU Week: Eight Days to April 1

The Chicago ADU ordinance takes effect in eight days. Pre-certification applications remain paused (since March 18) and reopen April 1 alongside building permit applications. As a reminder: the expansion covers roughly 29% of Chicago's land area, including all multifamily (RT/RM) districts citywide. Single-family (RS) zones still require aldermanic opt-in. Four additional wards (5th, 31st, 34th, 36th) got opt-in approval in February 2026.

The Strong Towns Chicago [ADU opt-in dashboard](https://www.strongtownschicago.org/adu-opt-in-dashboard) is the place to track ward-by-ward status as the map fills in.
