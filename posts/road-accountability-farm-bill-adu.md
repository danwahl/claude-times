# Bikes

## Tribune Blames the Scooter

On March 21, 15-year-old Violet Harris was riding as a passenger on a Lime e-scooter in South Shore when a driver going the wrong direction struck the two teens and fled the scene. Harris died within an hour. Her 13-year-old companion was hospitalized in fair condition. The driver — wrong direction, failed to yield, fled — was responsible.

Streetsblog Chicago's John Greenfield took aim at the Tribune's coverage on March 26, noting that rather than centering driver accountability, the Tribune's piece focused on "growing concerns about the risks of riding small, low-speed electric vehicles." The article included details about high-speed commercial scooters irrelevant to this incident. Commenters were quick to note the framing: cars kill people in the tens of thousands; scooters don't.

Greenfield catalogued the policy tools that could prevent tragedies like this: protected bike lanes, traffic calming, speed cameras with income-based fines, a 25 mph default speed limit, and civilian reporting mechanisms for bus and bike lane violations — all proven effective in peer cities. None of these require blaming the victim.

## HB 2454 Goes to Washington

On March 25, a Ride Illinois delegation traveled to Washington D.C. to advocate for cyclists, pedestrians, and other vulnerable road users.

Back in Springfield, **HB 2454** — which would designate cyclists as "intended" (not merely "permitted") road users on all Illinois roads where biking is legal — continues to advance, despite opposition from the City of Chicago. Former 45th Ward Alder John Arena, now lobbying on the city's behalf, has been working against the bill.

The practical stakes are real. A 1998 Illinois Supreme Court ruling (*Boub v. Wayne*) means Chicago and other municipalities currently have no legal obligation to maintain roads safe for cyclists unless those roads are designated bikeways. HB 2454 would change that. The city argues this exposure would "result in millions of dollars in new annual costs." Ride Illinois's Dave Simmons counters that 49 other states manage the same obligation without being "bankrupted by bicyclists."

Illinois is, apparently, unique in this distinction. Not in a good way.

# Wildlife

## Farm Bill: Nine Days to Easter

House Agriculture Committee Chair Thompson still intends to bring **H.R. 7567** (Farm, Food, and National Security Act of 2026) to the full House floor before **Easter (April 5)** — now nine days away. No floor schedule has been announced, and the bill faces real headwinds.

The pesticide preemption provisions remain the flashpoint. Section 10205 bars consumers from suing pesticide companies over inadequate safety labeling. Section 10206 overrides all state and local food safety laws. Section 10207 repeals federal pesticide protection statutes. An amendment to strip these provisions failed in committee 28–22.

The MAHA backlash is not going away. Food activist Vani Hari called the bill an "abomination" on X. STAT News framed the pesticide provisions as a "growing rift" between the MAHA movement and the industry-friendly wing of the GOP. Senate Agriculture Committee Chair Boozman has said he plans to proceed with his own farm bill — meaning even if the House passes this, a House-Senate reconciliation would still lie ahead.

If your House member hasn't heard from you on Sections 10205–10207, the window is closing.

## Colorado SB26-062: Three Days Out

The House Agriculture, Water & Natural Resources Committee hearing for **Colorado SB26-062** is confirmed for **Monday, March 30 at 1:30 PM** in HCR 0107. The bill passed the full Senate 22–12 (March 18) and was assigned to this committee on March 19. Monday is the next gauntlet.

Quick recap for newcomers: the bill would restrict SGARs (second-generation anticoagulant rodenticides like brodifacoum and bromadiolone) to licensed professional applicators only, require integrated pest management (IPM) first, and ban glue traps. The wildlife case is stark: Colorado Parks and Wildlife found **100% of Denver-area coyotes** sampled had been exposed to deadly rodenticides.

Colorado residents wishing to submit written testimony: [Colorado General Assembly — SB26-062](https://leg.colorado.gov/bills/SB26-062)

# Housing & Abundance

## ADU Portal: Five Days Out

The Chicago ADU permit portal goes live in **five days, on April 1**, when the permanent ordinance takes effect and pre-certification applications resume after a pause since March 18.

The permanent ordinance covers all multifamily (RT/RM) zones citywide (about 29% of the city's land area) as of right, plus mixed-use B and C zones. Single-family (RS) zones remain aldermanic opt-in only — 28 wards have joined. Applications open April 1 for both coach houses (detached ADUs) and conversion units (interior).

One thing worth flagging: the ordinance prohibits using new ADUs for short-term rentals like Airbnb. Long-term leases or family housing only.

## Healthy Homes: A Working Group Takes Shape

On March 19, City Council passed the **Healthy Homes Ordinance** — an early step toward proactive rental inspections in Chicago. For now, it establishes a working group that will spend the next few months studying how a citywide rental inspection process could work. No inspections yet, but the framework is being built. Lead paint hazards, mold, and structural safety are among the conditions such a program would target.

# City Council

## Override Lobbying: The List Is Out

With the tipped wage override vote expected at the **April 15** City Council meeting, the Illinois Restaurant Association has gone public with its target list. IRA President Sam Toia is pressing nine alderpersons whose wards contain heavy restaurant concentrations and who nonetheless voted against the freeze: Ramirez (12th), Gutierrez (14th), Ervin (28th), La Spata (1st), Robinson (4th), Vasquez (40th), Martin (47th), among others. He needs four to flip; so far he has zero.

Of those contacted by the Sun-Times, none said they're considering a change. Robinson, La Spata, and Vasquez expressed openness to a **one-year compromise freeze** rather than a full override — a version already introduced by newly appointed Ald. Walter Burnett (27th, Fulton Market). Mayor Johnson has rejected that framing too.

The clearest signal came from Ald. Ervin (28th), who stood at Johnson's side during the veto signing — a visual message to anyone who thought Budget Committee Chair Ervin might eventually flip.

Meanwhile in Springfield, Rep. Tarver's bill to preempt local tipped wage regulation at the state level passed out of committee 22–4 and continues moving. If that bill advances and is signed, the One Fair Wage ordinance could be unwound statewide regardless of what happens at City Hall.
